Command Words

The command word tells you exactly what the examiner wants. Getting this wrong loses marks instantly — describing when you should evaluate, or evaluating when you should describe.

❌ Most common error: Adding evaluation (strengths/weaknesses) to a describe question. Evaluation does not gain credit on AO1 questions — it wastes your time and earns zero marks.
Describe
AO1 · Knowledge
Give accurate, detailed information about what something is, how it works, or what was found.
✅ DO: Give facts, findings, procedures, components. Use specific details and correct terminology.
❌ DON'T: Add strengths, weaknesses or any evaluation. Do not say "a strength of this is..."
Outline
AO1 · Knowledge
A briefer form of describe — key points only, without extensive detail.
✅ DO: Cover 2-3 key points accurately. More breadth, less depth than a describe answer.
❌ DON'T: Write a full essay. Outline questions are usually lower marks (2-4).
Explain
AO1 · Knowledge
Give reasons — explain WHY or HOW something happens. Not just what, but why it is the case.
✅ DO: Always use "because", "this means that", "this is because". Link your knowledge to the question.
❌ DON'T: Just describe without giving reasons or making the link explicit.
Evaluate
AO3 · Evaluation
Assess strengths AND weaknesses. Give a judgement based on evidence.
✅ DO: Include BOTH strengths AND weaknesses. Use PEEL. Reach a conclusion. Use evidence.
❌ DON'T: Write only strengths OR only weaknesses — you cannot access 6+ marks with one side only.
Discuss
AO3 · Evaluation
Consider different aspects — present arguments on both sides of an issue.
✅ DO: Present FOR and AGAINST. Develop each argument. Reach an overall conclusion.
❌ DON'T: Argue only one side — you need both perspectives. Do not just list points without developing them.
Apply / With reference to the scenario
AO2 · Application
Use psychological knowledge in the context given. Every point must explicitly link to the scenario or character named in the question.
✅ DO: Use the name of the person/situation from the question in every paragraph. Generic answers score 1-2 marks maximum.
❌ DON'T: Write general psychology without linking to the scenario. Never ignore the scenario.
To what extent
AO3 · Evaluation
Take a position and justify it. How far do you agree — and why? You must reach a clear verdict.
✅ DO: Argue both sides, weigh up the evidence, and reach a clear evidence-based conclusion: "largely agree because..."
❌ DON'T: Sit on the fence. A conclusion that simply says "there are arguments on both sides" gains very few marks.
Identify
AO1 · Knowledge
Name it — usually with a brief explanation. Short answers only.
✅ DO: Name the thing being asked for. Add a sentence of explanation for 2-mark questions.
❌ DON'T: Write a paragraph. Identify questions are 2-3 marks maximum.

⚠️ Critical distinctions — common exam errors

"Describe the findings..."
Only findings. Conclusions gain NO credit. Don't write "this shows that..." or "Raine concluded..."
"Evaluate the ethical issues AND social implications..."
Both are required. Only covering ethical issues, however thoroughly, cannot score above 5/10.
"Describe the findings AND conclusions..."
Now you need both. The word "conclusions" explicitly appears. Covering only findings caps you at 5/10.
"Evaluate the strengths AND weaknesses..."
Both are required. Writing only strengths, however well, cannot score above 5/10. Both sides needed for top band.

AO1 — Describe Findings [8 marks]

Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of a study's findings.

~10 minutes AO1 only No evaluation

What does the examiner want?

Depth AND range — not just one finding in detail, and not a long list without explanation. You need several findings, each explained with specific detail and terminology.

For a findings-only question: conclusions gain NO credit. Do not write "Raine concluded..." or "this shows that..."

Use precise figures where possible (e.g. "approximately 170,000 people across 16 countries").

❌ Common mistakes

✗Adding conclusions: "This finding shows that happiness is determined by personality" — this is a conclusion and gains no credit on a findings-only question.
✗Adding evaluation: "A strength of this finding is..." — evaluation gains zero marks on AO1 questions.
✗Too narrow: Writing only one finding in detail — you need range (several different findings) as well as depth.
✓Correct: Report several distinct findings with specific statistics, naming the relevant comparisons (e.g. culture, age, money, traits).

Band descriptors (from the real mark scheme)

MarksWhat this looks like
7–8Thorough accuracy. Depth AND range. Effective terminology. Logical structure.
5–6Reasonable accuracy. Depth and range but not in equal measure. Good terminology.
3–4Basic accuracy. Depth OR range (not both). Some terminology.
1–2Superficial. Little terminology. Lacks structure.
⏱ Timing: 8 marks = approximately 10 minutes. If you are still writing after 12 minutes, move on.

Scaffold — How to structure your answer

📋 The real 2024 Q1: "Describe the findings of Myers and Diener's (1995) research 'Who is happy?'" [8] — Findings only. No conclusions.
Opening sentence — introduce the study briefly
"Myers and Diener (1995) conducted a meta-analytic review of studies of subjective wellbeing, including Inglehart's (1990) survey of approximately 170,000 people across 16 countries. The findings covered several demographic and personal factors..."
Finding 1 — Happiness and age / gender / race
"In terms of age, happiness levels showed no real difference across the lifespan, although the predictors of happiness changed with age. In terms of gender, there was also no significant difference overall, although women were found to be twice as vulnerable to depression and anxiety whilst men were five times more vulnerable to alcoholism."
Finding 2 — Culture
"In terms of culture, large differences were found. For example, in Portugal approximately 10% of people reported being very happy, compared to approximately 40% of people in the Netherlands."
Finding 3 — Money and traits
"The relationship between money and happiness showed only a moderate positive correlation. The traits most associated with happiness were self-esteem, a sense of personal control, optimism and extraversion."
Finding 4 — Relationships / Flow / Faith
"People with more friends and closer relationships reported higher levels of happiness and positive emotion. Those with greater work satisfaction also reported higher life satisfaction. Religious people reported higher levels of happiness than non-religious people."
Credit will be given for:
• Happiness and age — no real difference; predictors change
• Happiness and gender — generally no difference; women more vulnerable to depression, men to alcoholism
• Happiness and race — no real difference (Crocker & Major, 1989)
• Happiness and culture — large differences (Portugal ~10% vs Netherlands ~40%)
• Money and happiness — moderate positive correlation
• Traits of happy people — self-esteem, personal control, optimism, extraversion
• Relationships — more friends = happier, healthier, less likely to die prematurely
• Flow — work satisfaction linked to life satisfaction
• Faith — religious people report higher happiness

⚠️ N.B. Conclusions will not gain any credit.

AO1 — Describe Findings AND Conclusions [10 marks]

BOTH findings AND conclusions must be covered. Each alone caps you at 5/10.

~12 minutes AO1 only Both required
🚨 Critical rule from the real mark scheme: "Both findings and conclusions must be included to access the 6–10 mark range." Writing only findings OR only conclusions, however thoroughly, cannot score above 5/10.

Findings vs Conclusions — what is the difference?

FINDINGS

What was measured or observed in the study — the raw results. Numbers, comparisons, patterns in the data.

"The NGRI murderers showed reduced glucose metabolism in the prefrontal cortex compared to matched controls."

CONCLUSIONS

What the findings mean — what can be inferred or concluded from those results. The interpretation.

"Raine concluded there is a link between brain dysfunction and a predisposition to violence, but warned findings do not mean violence is biologically determined."

❌ Common mistakes

✗Writing only findings: Detailed findings but no conclusions — caps at 5/10 regardless of quality.
✗Confusing findings with conclusions: "Raine found that murderers were more likely to be violent" is a conclusion, not a finding.
✗Rushing conclusions: One-sentence conclusion after long findings section — aim for roughly half/half.
✓Correct: Structure your answer clearly — findings first, then conclusions. Use "Raine found that..." for findings and "Raine concluded that..." for conclusions.

Scaffold — Raine, Buchsbaum & LaCasse (1997)

📋 The real 2024 Q4: "Describe the findings and conclusions of Raine et al. (1997)." [10]
Introduce the study briefly
"Raine et al. (1997) used PET scanning to measure glucose metabolism — a marker of brain activity — in 41 NGRI murderers compared to 41 matched controls. The study examined 14 different brain areas."
Findings — brain areas with REDUCED activity in NGRIs
"The NGRI group showed reduced glucose metabolism in the prefrontal cortex (associated with impulse control and rational decision-making), the left amygdala, thalamus, hippocampus, and the angular gyrus (linked to morality)."
Findings — brain areas with INCREASED activity in NGRIs
"There was increased activity in the right amygdala, right thalamus, right hippocampus, and the cerebellum. There were no significant differences between NGRI and controls in areas linked to mental illness — differences were specific to regions associated with aggression and violence."
Conclusions (now switch to "concluded" / "suggested")
"Raine concluded there is a meaningful link between brain dysfunction and a predisposition towards violence in NGRIs. However, he warned that the findings do not show violent behaviour is determined by biology alone — social and psychological factors also play important roles. He also noted that the brain differences observed could be an effect of violent behaviour rather than a cause, since causation cannot be established from this correlational study. Furthermore, the findings do not mean murderers are not responsible for their actions, and PET scans cannot diagnose murderers."

AO1 — Describe a Therapy [10 marks]

Describe the main components of a therapy. No evaluation — depth AND range of components needed.

~12 minutes AO1 only Choose ONE therapy
⚠️ You choose ONE therapy. In 2024 Q6 students could choose CBT OR REBT. Pick the one you know better — both are worth equal marks. Don't try to cover both.

What does the examiner want?

Depth AND range of the therapy's components — how it works, the stages/techniques, the role of the therapist, the link to the approach's assumptions.

The mark scheme for Q6 (2024) credited: history of the therapy, link between assumptions and therapy, role of the therapist, specific techniques (e.g. dysfunctional thought diary, homework, cognitive restructuring), the ABC model (for REBT), the negative cognitive triad (for CBT).

CBT — Key components to describe

1. Link to assumptions
How assumptions justify CBT: "CBT is justified by the cognitive assumption that internal mental processes — particularly faulty schemas and negative automatic thoughts — cause emotional distress. If distorted thinking causes disorder, then challenging and changing that thinking should produce improvement."
2. Beck's negative cognitive triad
"Beck proposed that depression is maintained by a negative cognitive triad — negative views of the self ('I am worthless'), the world ('Nothing works out') and the future ('Things will never improve'). CBT aims to challenge all three."
3. Dysfunctional thought diary
"The client keeps a dysfunctional thought diary, recording negative automatic thoughts as they occur, along with the situations that trigger them. This makes unconscious thinking patterns visible and subject to challenge."
4. Cognitive restructuring
"The therapist challenges negative automatic thoughts by examining the evidence for and against them, and helping the client replace irrational beliefs with more balanced, realistic ones."
5. Behavioural activation and homework
"Clients are given homework tasks between sessions — scheduling positive activities, testing predictions about feared situations, and practising new thinking patterns in real life. This applies the learning beyond the therapy room."

Systematic Desensitisation — Key components

1. Link to conditioning assumptions
"SD is justified by the classical conditioning assumption: if phobias are conditioned fear responses (CS → CR), then pairing the feared stimulus with an incompatible response (relaxation) should extinguish the fear through counter-conditioning."
2. Anxiety hierarchy
"Client and therapist construct a ranked hierarchy of feared stimuli — from least to most frightening. For a spider phobia, this might begin with the word 'spider' and end with holding a live spider."
3. Relaxation training
"The client learns a deep relaxation response — controlled breathing, progressive muscle relaxation — which is physiologically incompatible with anxiety. Anxiety and relaxation cannot coexist."
4. Gradual exposure / Counter-conditioning
"The client works through the hierarchy, pairing each level with the relaxation response until no fear reaction remains. The CS (spider) now produces CR (relaxation) instead of CR (fear) — this is counter-conditioning."

Dream Analysis — Key components

1. Link to unconscious mind assumption
"Dream analysis is justified by the psychodynamic assumption that the unconscious mind contains repressed conflicts driving behaviour. During sleep, the ego weakens, allowing unconscious material to surface — but it is disguised by defence mechanisms."
2. Manifest and latent content
"The manifest content is what the dreamer actually remembers — the surface narrative. The latent content is the true unconscious meaning, disguised through mechanisms such as displacement (substituting one object for another), condensation (merging several images), and symbolism."
3. Free association
"The therapist uses free association — asking the patient to say whatever comes to mind in response to dream elements — to decode the latent content from the manifest content."
4. Making the unconscious conscious
"By bringing the unconscious material into conscious awareness, the patient can confront and resolve the underlying conflict, reducing the neurotic symptoms it causes."

Mindfulness — Key components

1. Link to positive approach assumptions
"Mindfulness is justified by the positive approach assumptions: acknowledging free will means individuals can actively cultivate present-moment awareness; focusing on the good life means developing a non-judgemental, accepting relationship with experience."
2. Core practices
"Core mindfulness practices include body scan meditation (systematic attention to bodily sensations), mindful breathing (sustained focus on the breath as anchor), and observing thoughts without judgement — noticing without engaging."
3. MBCT
"Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT, Segal, Williams and Teasdale, 2002) combines mindfulness with cognitive techniques from CBT. It is NICE-recommended for prevention of depressive relapse in people who have experienced three or more depressive episodes."
4. Programme structure
"A typical MBCT programme runs for 8 weeks, with weekly group sessions and daily home practice. Participants develop present-moment awareness and a different relationship to negative thoughts — seeing them as mental events rather than facts."

AO3 — Evaluate Strengths and Weaknesses [8 or 10 marks]

Both strengths AND weaknesses needed to access 6+ marks. Use PEEL structure throughout.

8 or 10 marks AO3 only Both sides essential
🚨 Critical rule from both Q2 and Q3 mark schemes: "Both strengths and weaknesses must be included to access the 6–10 mark range." A one-sided answer, however detailed, cannot score above 5 marks.

What does the examiner want?

Developed, evidence-backed evaluation points — not a list. Each point should be P-E-E-L: state it, support it with evidence, explain why it matters, link back to the question.

A conclusion is required for the top band. "On balance, the biological approach is [strong/limited] because..."

Points can be evaluated as either a strength OR weakness as long as they are justified — the mark scheme allows flexibility here.

Band descriptors — 10-mark evaluate (Q2, 2024)

6–8Reasonable evaluation. Appropriate examples. Developed. Mostly logical. Reasonable conclusion.

The PEEL Paragraph — the essential tool for AO3

P
Point
State your argument in one clear sentence. Name whether it is a strength or weakness.
"A strength/weakness of the biological approach is..."
E
Evidence
Name a specific study, researcher, or piece of evidence. Include specific detail — not just "research shows".
"Raine et al. (1997) used PET scanning to compare 41 NGRI murderers with 41 matched controls, finding reduced prefrontal cortex activity..."
E
Explain
Explain WHY this is a strength/weakness. What does the evidence mean? Why does it matter?
"This is a strength because it demonstrates that psychological phenomena can be studied using objective, measurable methods, lending the approach scientific credibility..."
L
Link
Link back to the question. What does this point tell us about the approach/therapy overall?
"Therefore the biological approach can be considered [scientific / reductionist / deterministic], which means..."

Model answer — Evaluate biological approach [10 marks, Q2 2024]

STRENGTHA significant strength of the biological approach is its use of objective, scientific methods. Raine et al. (1997) used PET scanning — a highly controlled neuroimaging technique — to compare brain activity in 41 NGRI murderers with 41 matched controls. The use of matched controls and a standardised task increases the internal validity of the findings. This is a strength because it demonstrates that psychological phenomena can be studied with measurable, replicable methods, helping to establish psychology as a scientific discipline.

STRENGTHA further strength is the real-world applications the approach has generated. Understanding the role of neurotransmitters — particularly serotonin in depression and dopamine in schizophrenia — has led to the development of drug therapies such as SSRIs and antipsychotics. These treatments have allowed millions of people to manage mental illness and live more independently. Viguera et al. (2000) found that over 60% of bipolar patients improved with lithium, demonstrating the practical value of biologically-based treatments.

WEAKNESSHowever, a major weakness is that the biological approach is reductionist, reducing complex human behaviour entirely to biological factors such as genes, brain structures and neurotransmitters. Explaining depression purely as "low serotonin" ignores the role of negative life events, cognitive patterns and social support. This is a weakness because it provides an incomplete account of behaviour and can lead to treatments — like drug therapy — that address symptoms without tackling underlying causes, contributing to the "revolving door syndrome" where patients relapse when medication is stopped.

WEAKNESSThe approach is also criticised for biological determinism — the idea that behaviour is fixed by biology, leaving no room for free will or individual agency. This raises serious ethical concerns: Raine et al.'s findings about murderers' brain differences could be used to argue that violent offenders are not responsible for their actions, which has significant implications for justice and punishment. Raine himself resisted this conclusion, acknowledging that social and psychological factors also contribute.

On balance, the biological approach has made genuine and valuable contributions through its scientific rigour and real-world applications. However, its reductionist and deterministic stance means it provides only a partial account of human behaviour, and it is most effectively used alongside other approaches that account for thoughts, environment and culture.

AO3 — Evaluate Ethical Issues AND Social Implications [10 marks]

BOTH ethical issues AND social implications must be included to access 6+ marks.

10 marks AO3 only Both types required
🚨 From the real mark scheme Q7: "Both ethical issues and social implications must be included to access the 6–10 mark range." A thorough answer covering only ethical issues cannot score above 5/10.

Ethical issues vs Social implications — the difference

⚖️ Ethical issues

Problems with HOW the research was conducted — consent, deception, harm, right to withdraw.

"Participants were deceived about the true aim of the study — informed consent was not fully obtained."

🌍 Social implications

What the research MEANS for society — criminal justice, policy, public understanding, real-world impact.

"The Innocence Project found approximately 70% of wrongful convictions involved mistaken EWT — demonstrating the devastating social cost of unreliable testimony."

Scaffold — Loftus and Palmer (1974) Q7, 2024

📋 The real 2024 Q7: "Evaluate the ethical issues and social implications of Loftus and Palmer's (1974) research." [10]
Ethical Issue 1 — Deception
"A key ethical issue is deception. Participants were not informed of the true aim of the study — that it was investigating how question wording alters memory. Therefore informed consent was not fully obtained. Although a debrief was provided, participants had still been led to form false memories without their knowledge. The BPS permits deception only where it does not cause harm and a full debrief is given — Loftus and Palmer satisfied the latter but the use of deception still raises concerns about autonomy."
Ethical Issue 2 — Potential harm / Right to withdraw
"A further concern is potential harm from watching distressing accident footage. The use of film clips rather than real accidents reduced this risk, but participants may still have experienced some distress. There are also concerns about the right to withdraw — participants were student volunteers in an institutional setting, which may have created a power imbalance making it difficult to leave freely."
Social Implication 1 — Criminal justice / Cognitive Interview
"A major social implication is the reform of police interviewing. The research directly contributed to the development of the Cognitive Interview (Geiselman et al., 1985) — now used by UK police forces — which produces more accurate and detailed recall. This is a positive social impact: justice is better served when evidence is more reliably obtained."
Social Implication 2 — Wrongful convictions / The Innocence Project
"The most significant social implication is the role of unreliable EWT in wrongful convictions. The Innocence Project found that approximately 70% of wrongful convictions overturned by DNA evidence had involved mistaken eyewitness identification. These cases demonstrate the devastating human cost — innocent people imprisoned, real perpetrators free, families destroyed. As a result, EWT is now used alongside forensic evidence and CCTV rather than as the sole basis for conviction."
Conclusion
"On balance, while Loftus and Palmer's research raised ethical concerns through deception, its social impact has been largely positive — improving interviewing practice, reforming how courts weigh EWT, and generating the evidence base for the Innocence Project's work."

Scaffold — Compare Cognitive and Positive approaches (Q5, 2024)

📋 The real 2024 Q5: A student asked whether to study cognitive or positive psychology at university. Discuss what Mr Haggerty (the teacher) may advise by comparing and contrasting the two approaches. [10]
Similarity 1 — both use scientific methods (link to scenario)
"Mr Haggerty might begin by noting a key similarity: both the cognitive and positive approaches use scientific, empirical methods to study behaviour. The cognitive approach uses controlled laboratory experiments — as in Loftus and Palmer (1974) — while the positive approach uses large-scale surveys and meta-analyses, such as Myers and Diener's (1995) review of 170,000 people. For a student interested in rigorous research at university, both approaches offer credible scientific foundations."
Difference 1 — what they study (link to scenario)
"However, the teacher might also note a fundamental difference in focus. The cognitive approach studies how faulty mental processes — such as negative schemas and cognitive distortions — cause disorder. The positive approach studies what makes people flourish — happiness, strengths and meaning. For the student deciding her future, Mr Haggerty might advise that if she is interested in clinical work or understanding why people become mentally ill, the cognitive approach is more relevant; if she is interested in wellbeing promotion, education or coaching, the positive approach would be a better fit."
Difference 2 — therapies / determinism (link to scenario)
"A further difference concerns their therapies and view of free will. The cognitive approach's CBT involves soft determinism — acknowledging that thought patterns shape behaviour but can be changed through reflection. The positive approach explicitly assumes free will — Seligman argued people can actively choose to cultivate strengths. Mr Haggerty might advise that this philosophical difference matters for the student: does she believe people can truly choose their wellbeing, or are they influenced by prior cognitive patterns?"
Conclusion (link back to scenario)
"Ultimately, Mr Haggerty might advise that neither approach is objectively better — both have produced NICE-recommended therapies (CBT and mindfulness). The student's choice should depend on whether her interests lie in understanding the causes of disorder (cognitive) or in promoting human flourishing (positive)."

Research to justify the therapy's effectiveness can also be included to support the AO2 application.

Scaffold — Q8, 2024: Why would a psychodynamic psychologist use dream analysis?

📋 The real 2024 Q8: "Discuss why a psychologist from the psychodynamic approach would consider dream analysis OR psychodrama as an appropriate therapy." [10]
Assumption 1 → Therapy link
"A psychologist from the psychodynamic approach would use dream analysis because the unconscious mind assumption holds that repressed conflicts and unacceptable desires — invisible to conscious awareness — drive neurotic behaviour. During sleep, the ego weakens and the id strengthens, meaning these unconscious contents can surface. Dream analysis provides access to this material that no other method can reach."
Assumption 2 → Therapy link (defence mechanisms)
"The approach also assumes that the mind uses defence mechanisms (displacement, condensation, symbolism) to disguise unconscious material — which is why the latent content of a dream is hidden within the manifest content. Dream analysis is therefore necessary because the unconscious does not simply reveal itself directly. The therapist's role is to decode the symbolism through free association."
Assumption 3 → Therapy link (childhood experiences)
"Furthermore, because the psychodynamic approach assumes that unresolved childhood experiences cause adult neurosis, dream analysis provides a route to those buried early memories. By helping the patient bring the unconscious conflict into conscious awareness — what Freud called 'making the unconscious conscious' — the therapy allows the conflict to be resolved rather than merely suppressed."
Evidence to justify
"A psychodynamic psychologist might also point to Freud's own case studies — including Little Hans and Anna O — as evidence that uncovering unconscious material through dream analysis and free association can produce significant improvements in neurotic symptoms, justifying the use of this therapy."

24-Mark Contemporary Debate Essay

12 AO1 (knowledge of both sides) + 12 AO3 (evaluation: ethical, social, economic). Evidence-based conclusion. ~28 minutes.

24 marks AO1 (12) + AO3 (12) Both sides required ESE framework

What makes a top-band 24-marker?

1
Both sides of the debate — arguments FOR and AGAINST the statement. AO1 knowledge of both positions. Cannot reach top band covering one side only.
2
Ethical, Social AND Economic perspectives in your AO3. Use the ESE framework. All three are needed for top band evaluation.
3
Evidence throughout — name specific researchers, studies and statistics. "Research shows..." without a name gains low marks.
4
Clear conclusion — "On balance, I largely agree/disagree because..." Take a position. Do not simply say "there are arguments on both sides."
5
Cultural diversity — consider whether findings apply across cultures. At least one sentence mentioning cross-cultural considerations pushes AO3 to top band.
🚨 AO3 from the real mark scheme: "Thorough discussion of BOTH sides of the debate. Evaluative comments evidently relevant to the context. Structure logical throughout. Appropriate conclusion based on analysing evidence." — This is what 10–12 AO3 looks like.

Timing breakdown

5
minutes planning — mini-plan: both sides, 4 PEEL points, conclusion position
23
minutes writing — intro + 4-5 paragraphs + conclusion
⏱ At 1.2 minutes per mark, Q9 needs 28-30 minutes. If you start Q9 with less than 25 minutes left, you will not do it justice.

Essay Structure — paragraph by paragraph

INTRO
Introduction (~3-4 sentences)
State what the debate is about. Give your overall position. Briefly mention the key evidence you will use.
"[State the debate]. I largely [agree/disagree] with the statement because [brief reason]. However, [acknowledge other side], which means the question requires careful consideration."
AO1 FOR
Paragraph 1 — Knowledge supporting the statement
Evidence, research and arguments that support the view expressed in the statement. Name specific researchers and studies.
"One argument that supports the statement is... [researcher/study] found that... This supports the statement because..."
AO3 + ⚖️
Paragraph 2 — Ethical evaluation
Evaluate using an ethical perspective. Use an ethical principle (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, consent).
"Ethically, [the statement/this position] raises concerns about [principle] because..." | "This is ethically justifiable because it upholds [principle] by..."
AO1+AO3 AGAINST
Paragraph 3 — Knowledge challenging + Social evaluation
Evidence that challenges the statement, combined with a social perspective on the implications.
"However, [researcher] challenges the statement by... Socially, this has implications for [group/institution/policy] because..."
AO3 + 💰
Paragraph 4 — Economic evaluation
Economic perspective — costs, access, funding, inequality, savings to the NHS or criminal justice system.
"Economically, [the statement] has implications for... because... The financial cost of [X] means that..."
CONC
Conclusion (~3-4 sentences)
Take a clear overall position based on the evidence. Acknowledge the complexity but reach a verdict.
"On balance, I largely [agree/disagree] with the statement. The evidence from [X] demonstrates [Y]. However, [counter-argument] means the position must be qualified. Overall, [final verdict] because [key reason]."

The ESE Framework — Ethical · Social · Economic

All three perspectives should appear in your AO3 evaluation. Use these sentence starters to structure each evaluative paragraph.

⚖️ Ethical

Key principles: autonomy, informed consent, beneficence (doing good), non-maleficence (doing no harm), justice.

"Ethically, this raises concerns about autonomy because..."

"This violates the principle of non-maleficence because it causes harm to..."

"From an ethical standpoint, this is justifiable because it upholds beneficence by..."

🌍 Social

Consider: impact on specific groups, policy implications, inequality, criminal justice, cultural diversity, stereotyping.

"Socially, this has significant implications because it could lead to..."

"This raises social concerns about inequality because..."

"From a social perspective, this challenges the traditional view that... meaning..."

"Cultural diversity must also be considered — in collectivist cultures..."

💰 Economic

Consider: NHS costs, cost of wrongful convictions, childcare costs, productivity, access to expensive treatments.

"Economically, this is significant because the cost of [X] is estimated at..."

"The economic concern is that only those who can afford... will benefit, meaning..."

"Economically, investing in [X] is cost-effective because it reduces the long-term burden on..."

Model plan — Q9, 2024: "It is not essential that the mother is the primary caregiver of an infant." Discuss.

My position (decide before writing)
I largely disagree — the evidence does not support the idea that the mother specifically is essential, though early secure attachment is.
AO1 FOR the statement (mother IS essential)
• Bowlby (1944) — 44 thieves, 86% affectionless psychopaths had maternal separation
• Monotropy — one primary qualitatively unique attachment, usually mother
• Internal working model — template for all future relationships
• Biological: oxytocin release; NHS breastfeeding guidance
AO1 AGAINST the statement (mother NOT essential)
• Rutter (1981) — privation vs deprivation; Czech twins recovered
• Schaffer & Emerson (1964) — 29% primary attachment not the mother; multiple attachments
• Lamb (1997) — fathers equally effective as primary caregivers
• Cross-cultural: kibbutzim; Tronick et al. Efe people
AO3 — Ethical
The statement is ethically problematic because it restricts women's autonomy and ignores gender equality. It places the burden of childcare on women — Ann Oakley argued Bowlby's theory was used post-war to push women out of the workforce.
AO3 — Social
Socially, the statement excludes same-sex couples and adoptive families. NICHD (2003) — high-quality day care produces equivalent outcomes. Cultural diversity: communal caregiving in Efe and kibbutzim challenges the Western assumption.
AO3 — Economic
Economically, insisting on maternal care removes millions of women from the workforce, reducing GDP, tax revenue and household income. UK childcare costs among highest in developed world — economic case for quality shared care.
Conclusion
"On balance, I largely disagree. Bowlby was right that early secure attachment is essential, but the weight of evidence — Rutter, Schaffer & Emerson, and cross-cultural research — shows the caregiver does not need to be the biological mother. What infants need is a sensitive, responsive and consistent caregiver, regardless of gender or biological relationship."

Bowlby's attachment theory and his concept of monotropy propose that infants form one primary, uniquely important bond — typically with the mother — during a critical period, and that disrupting this causes lasting emotional damage. I largely disagree with the statement that the mother must be the primary caregiver. While early secure attachment is undeniably important, the evidence suggests it is the quality and consistency of caregiving that matters, not the identity of the caregiver. There are also strong ethical, social and economic reasons to challenge the statement.

🎯 Critical misconceptions flagged in 2022

❌ "Little Albert's mother did not consent" — WRONG. She did consent. Little Albert's mother also removed him from the study — so there WAS a right to withdraw.
❌ "The gender imbalance in Raine et al. is a weakness" — WRONG. The sample reflected the actual prison population — all male NGRI offenders. This is not a sampling flaw.
❌ "The positive approach is on the side of nature/nurture" — WRONG. The positive approach assumes free will — which means freedom from BOTH biological (nature) and environmental (nurture) determinism.
❌ "Determinism and reductionism are the same thing" — WRONG. Determinism = behaviour is caused by prior factors beyond our control. Reductionism = complex behaviour is explained by one level of analysis only.
